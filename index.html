Why does Trump still dominate political social media in 2026?

We scored 89,325 posts from Donald Trump's Twitter (2009–2021) and Truth Social (2022–2026) accounts against his own usual performance. Posts written in his own words get a third more engagement than his typical post; posts that share a link get about a third less. How he says something matters more than what he says it about.

How to read this report

1.0×

Every post is compared with Trump's own usual at the time. His audience grew from a few thousand followers in 2009 to tens of millions, so raw likes mostly tell you when a post was made. We take the median engagement of his own posts in the 14 days either side as "usual". A post at 2.0× got twice his usual; 0.5× got half.

Engagement means likes plus reposts, the measure both platforms share. It comes from people who follow and see his posts, so it is a guide to what his core audience responds to, not a poll of his voters.

Key findings

  1. He out-posts almost everyone. About 540 posts a month in his second term. In early 2020 he posted 959 times a month on Twitter, seven to thirteen times the typical member of Congress.
  2. His own words beat links, clips and reposts. Holding topic and style constant, a post that is only an article link gets 33% less engagement than a comparable text post; on Twitter, links and media got 29% less.
  3. A recognisable voice is a measurable asset. CAPITALS add 13% on Truth Social and 34% on Twitter; exclamation marks add 19%; nicknames for opponents add 6–8%, all else equal.
  4. Values content outperforms; endorsements do not. Posts on patriotism, faith and national holidays get 21–29% more. Endorsing other candidates gets 34–39% less, yet made up 7% of his second-term posts.
  5. Owning a platform traded reach for control. His typical first-term tweet got about 89,600 likes and reposts; his typical Truth Social post gets about 16,500. Only 2% of US adults regularly get news on Truth Social.
  6. The 2026 Trump writes longer and reposts less. Reposts fell from 37% of his first-term feed to 9.5% in his second term. 27% of second-term posts run past 50 words, and those do 9% better.

What makes Trump work on social?

Seventeen years, two platforms, and almost no days off

Volume is the foundation. Trump has posted about 89,000 times since 2009, peaking at 1,415 posts in October 2020.

His output tracks his political career. He posted fewer than 10 times a day before his 2015 campaign launch, 14 a day during the campaign, 18 a day in his first term and 23 a day on Truth Social between 2022 and 2024, when he was out of office and campaigning. In his second term he has settled at about 18 a day.

His audience grew far faster than his posting, which is why we compare each post with his own usual.

A typical original post earned about 90 likes and reposts before the 2015 campaign, about 6,900 during it and about 89,600 in his first term. On Truth Social, a typical post earns about 16,500. The chart is on a log scale: each step up is ten times the one below.

No member of Congress comes close on volume.

In the first five months of 2020, Trump posted 959 times a month on Twitter, retweets included. In Pew Research Center's analysis of Congress to May 2020, the median Democratic member tweeted 130 times in a typical month and the median Republican 73. Pew also found that the top 10% of members by followers received more than three-quarters of all engagement, so attention concentrates on the few accounts that post most and draw the largest audiences.

What lands with his base, and what falls flat?

His audience rewards his voice and ignores his links

On Truth Social, posts in his own words get 1.33 times his usual engagement. Every other format falls below his usual.

Bare article links get 0.72×, shared posts from other accounts 0.70×, video links 0.86×, and images or videos posted with no caption 0.89×. Text-only posts make up 44% of his original posts, but 87% of his top 10%. On Twitter, from his 2015 campaign onwards, text posts also led (1.17×) while links, media and quoted fans fell below usual.

Holding everything else constant, style and format move engagement more than topic does.

Simple averages can mislead: if most link posts are about the economy, "links do badly" and "the economy does badly" look the same. A regression separates them. It estimates each factor's effect while holding the others fixed. The orange bars are the elements of his recognisable voice. Most topics move engagement by 12% or less; the exceptions are endorsements (−34%), sport and culture (−25%) and patriotism, faith and holidays (+21%).

In his second term, foreign policy and attacks on opponents are over-represented among his best posts. Endorsements are under-represented.

Foreign policy and the military appear in 22% of his second-term posts but 38% of his top 10% by engagement. Opponents appear in 12% of posts and 27% of the top 10%. Patriotism, faith and holidays rise from 2.5% to 6.8%. Endorsements of other candidates fall from 7.4% to 3.0%. A post can touch several topics, so shares add up to more than 100%.

A second, independent labelling of his Truth Social posts tells the same story: emotion and conflict outperform praise and neutral statements.

The Truth Social file came with its own labels for tone and rhetoric, made by a different method from ours. On those labels, posts marked negative get 1.40× his usual and positive posts 1.21×, against 0.80× for neutral posts. Posts labelled as praise of others get 0.93×; those labelled as accusations, denials or policy statements get 1.18–1.33×. Because the two labelling methods agree, we are more confident in the pattern.

A caution on "the base". These figures measure the reactions of people who follow and see Trump's posts. On Truth Social that audience is small and committed: Pew finds 2% of US adults regularly get news there. It is a good guide to his core supporters, not to everyone who voted for him. The coalition section below uses polling to describe the wider group.

The 2026 Trump posts longer, reposts less and posts earlier

What has changed is the packaging. What still works is the same: his own voice, emotion, CAPITALS and few links.

Between his first campaign and his second term, the typical post grew from 19 to 31 words, and more than a quarter now run past 50 words. Reposts of supporters, 37% of his first-term feed, are down to under 10%. A quarter of his posts are now images or videos with no caption, a format that did not exist in his Twitter archive. His best-performing hour moved from mid-afternoon to the morning.

The style signature has strengthened on Truth Social.

Each cell below compares posts with a feature against posts without it in the same period. Nicknames for opponents lifted engagement by 23% in his first term and by about half on Truth Social. Since 2017, links have cost him a third or more of his usual engagement in every period.

How does he compare?

A top-three world leader for X views, but ninth for engagement, because his main channel is now his own

On X, Trump had 15.2 million engagements between January and October 2026, ninth in Knox Digital's comparison of world leaders (which also includes UK MP Rupert Lowe) and less than a sixth of India's Narendra Modi.

Knox Digital's tracking of world leaders places him among the top three for views on X over the same period, with Modi and Japan's Sanae Takaichi. The gap between views and engagement fits what the files show: since 2022 he has posted mainly on Truth Social, where engagement is concentrated among his core audience, and his posts reach X and other networks largely when they are reshared or reported.

Most Americans get news on the big general platforms, not on the one Trump owns.

In 2026, 40% of US adults regularly get news on Facebook, 34% on YouTube, 23% on Instagram and 20% on TikTok. X stands at 10% and Truth Social at 2%. Pew finds Republicans slightly more likely than Democrats to get news on Facebook and X, and Democrats more likely on Instagram, TikTok and Reddit. YouTube is even.

His playbook now travels across parties.

Senior figures in both parties have adopted parts of it. Pew's study of Congress found that mentioning the other side's leading figures lifted engagement for Democrats and Republicans alike, consistent with our finding that opponent posts beat his usual. Two Democratic cases show the range: one copied his tone, the other his directness in a different format.

Governor of California · Democrat

Gavin Newsom's press office copied the tone

From August 2025 the office's X account adopted what ABC7 described as "all caps, MAGA phrases" and personal praise in Trump's style. The office reported 250,000 new followers that month and an engagement rate above 50%. A UC Berkeley poll for the LA Times found 59% of California voters wanted him to keep going.

Figures from Newsom's office, reported by ABC7 Los Angeles, August 2025.

Mayor of New York City · Democrat

Zohran Mamdani copied the directness, on video

His 2025 campaign relied on direct-to-camera short videos filmed on the street, including one explaining ranked-choice voting in Hindi. By the June 2025 primary he had more than 1 million Instagram followers, and one "Subway Takes" TikTok passed 3 million views.

NBC News, 26 June 2025.

Members of Congress · both parties

Opponents drive engagement on both sides

Posts mentioning prominent figures from the other party received "a substantial boost" in engagement for Democrats and Republicans alike. The median Republican member got more engagement per post; Democrats got 73% of all likes on Twitter in early 2020, because they posted more and had more large accounts.

Pew Research Center, 16 July 2020.

Trump himself · Republican

Owned platform: control over reach

Truth Social lets him post without depending on another company's rules and keeps his audience on his own platform. The trade-off in his own data: a typical post earns about a fifth of a typical first-term tweet, and only 2% of adults use the platform for news.

Datasets provided by ADI; Pew Research Center, 2026.

What can other politicians learn?

Eight lessons that hold for social media in 2026

These are communication tactics measured in Trump's data, set against how platforms and audiences work today. They describe what draws engagement, not whether any message is right. Each comes with the evidence and the main caution.

Trump's 2024 coalition was broader than his 2016 one, so one feed cannot reach all of it.

Pew's study of validated 2024 voters shows the coalition widened. White voters without a degree fell from 63% to 51% of his voters between 2016 and 2024. Voters under 50 rose to 40%. Hispanic voters rose from 6% to 10%, and men under 50 split almost evenly after backing Biden by ten points in 2020.
