What still works for Trump on social media in 2026?

We analysed 89,325 posts from Donald Trump's Twitter (2009–2021) and Truth Social (2022–2026) accounts, and scored the 49,155 original posts from his June 2015 campaign launch onwards against his own usual performance. Text-only posts get a third more engagement than his typical post; a post that is just an article link gets 0.72× his usual. How he says something matters more than what he says it about.

How to read this report

1.0×

Every post is compared with Trump's own usual at the time. His audience grew from a few thousand followers in 2009 to tens of millions, so raw likes mostly tell you when a post was made. We take the median engagement of his own posts in the 14 days either side as "usual". A post at 2.0× got twice his usual; 0.5× got half.

Engagement means likes plus reposts, the measure both platforms share. It comes from people who follow and see his posts, so it is a guide to what his core audience responds to, not a poll of his voters.

What makes Trump work on social?

Seventeen years, two platforms, and almost no days off

Volume is the foundation. Trump has posted about 89,000 times since 2009, peaking at 1,415 posts in October 2020.

His output tracks his political career. He posted fewer than 10 times a day before his 2015 campaign launch, 14 a day during the campaign, 18 a day in his first term and 23 a day on Truth Social between 2022 and 2024, when he was out of office and campaigning. In his second term he has settled at about 18 a day. He posted on 99% of days from June 2015 to January 2021, and on 95% of days on Truth Social.

His audience grew far faster than his posting, which is why we compare each post with his own usual.

A typical original post earned about 90 likes and reposts before the 2015 campaign, about 6,900 during it and about 90,400 in his first term. On Truth Social in his second term, a typical post earns about 16,600. The chart is on a log scale: each step up is ten times the one below.

No member of Congress comes close on volume.

In the first five months of 2020, Trump posted 959 times a month on Twitter including retweets, 436 of them his own. In Pew Research Center's analysis of Congress to May 2020, the median Democratic member tweeted 130 times in a typical month and the median Republican 73. Pew also found that the top 10% of members by followers received more than three-quarters of all engagement, so attention concentrates on the few accounts that post most and draw the largest audiences.

What lands with his base, and what falls flat?

His audience rewards text and ignores his links

On Truth Social, text-only posts get 1.33 times his usual engagement. Every other format falls below his usual.

Bare article links get 0.72×, shared posts from other accounts 0.70×, video links 0.86×, and images or videos posted with no caption 0.89×. Text-only posts make up 44% of his original posts, but 87% of his top 10%. On Twitter, from his 2015 campaign onwards, text posts also led (1.17×) while links, media and quoted fans fell below usual.

Holding everything else constant, style and format move engagement more than topic does.

Simple averages can mislead: if most link posts are about the economy, "links do badly" and "the economy does badly" look the same. A regression separates them. It estimates each factor's effect while holding the others fixed. The orange bars are the elements of his recognisable voice. On Truth Social, most topics move engagement by 10% or less; the exceptions are endorsements (−29%), sport and culture (−22%) and patriotism, faith and holidays (+17%). The model also holds constant how many posts he made that day: each doubling is linked to 15% less engagement per post on Truth Social and 4% less on Twitter.

Foreign policy fills a large share of his best second-term posts because those posts are long and text-only, not because of the topic. Compared with posts of the same kind, it underperforms.

Foreign policy and the military appear in 22% of his second-term posts but 38% of his top 10% by engagement. A typical foreign-policy post runs 89 words, against 11 for his other posts (which include captionless images and videos), and 84% are text-only. Comparing long posts only, foreign policy gets 1.09× his usual and other long posts 1.50×. Holding length, format and style constant, the regression puts foreign policy at 0.98×: no effect of its own. Posts about opponents appear in 12% of posts and 27% of the top 10%. Patriotism, faith and holidays rise from 2.5% to 6.9%. Endorsements of other candidates fall from 7.4% to 2.9%. A post can touch several topics, so shares add up to more than 100%.

The Truth Social file's own labels agree with ours on emotion and conflict, but not on praise.

The file came with its own labels for tone and rhetoric, made by a different, undocumented method. On those labels, posts marked negative get 1.40× his usual and positive posts 1.21×, against 0.80× for neutral posts. Accusations, denials and policy statements get 1.18–1.33×, in line with our finding that posts about opponents beat his usual. On praise the two methods disagree: posts the file labels as praise get 0.93×, while our "Thanks & self-promotion" topic gets 1.28×. One possible reason is that our topic also catches thanks and boasts about himself, but without the file's method we cannot confirm it. So we treat emotion and conflict as confirmed by both methods, and praise as unresolved.

A caution on "the base". These figures measure the reactions of people who follow and see Trump's posts. On Truth Social that audience is small and committed: Pew finds 2% of US adults regularly get news there. It is a good guide to his core supporters, not to everyone who voted for him. The coalition section below uses polling to describe the wider group.

The 2026 Trump posts longer and reposts less

What has changed is the packaging. What still works is the same: text-only posts, emotion, CAPITALS and few links.

Between his first campaign and his second term, the typical post grew from 19 to 31 words, and more than a quarter now run past 50 words. Reposts of supporters, 37% of his first-term feed, are down to under 10%. A quarter of his posts are now images or videos with no caption. The Twitter archive cannot show how often he did this, because every media tweet carried a t.co link. His best-performing hour moved from mid-afternoon to the morning, though he posts at much the same times (median 3pm ET) and timing itself makes little difference once content is held constant.

Style still matters, but much less than the raw gaps suggest.

Each cell below compares posts with a feature against posts without it in the same period. On those raw comparisons, nicknames for opponents lifted engagement by 23% in his first term and by about half on Truth Social. But the posts that use them are different: on Truth Social, posts with a nickname run a median of 68 words against 15, and 94% are text-only against 41%. Holding length, format and topic constant, a nickname adds 8% on both platforms, CAPITALS 15% on Truth Social and 33% on Twitter, and exclamation marks 16% and 5%. Since 2017, links have cost him a third or more of his usual engagement in every period.

How does he compare?

A top-three world leader for X views, but ninth for engagement, because his main channel is now his own

On X, Trump had 15.2 million engagements between January and October 2026, ninth in Knox Digital's comparison of world leaders (which also includes UK MP Rupert Lowe) and less than a sixth of India's Narendra Modi.

Knox Digital's tracking of world leaders places him among the top three for views on X over the same period, with Modi and Japan's Sanae Takaichi. The gap between views and engagement fits what the files show: since 2022 he has posted mainly on Truth Social, where engagement is concentrated among his core audience, and his posts reach X and other networks largely when they are reshared or reported.

Among senior US politicians, Trump's following is far larger than anyone else's: almost nine times the next largest on X.

We checked six senior figures, three from each party, on their public profiles on 26 November 2026. Trump has 111.7 million followers on X against 12.6 million for Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, the next largest; on Instagram the gap narrows to almost five times. His X following is more than eight times his Truth Social following, the platform where he now mainly posts, and Zohran Mamdani has about three times as many followers on Instagram as on X. Followers measure potential reach, not how many people see, engage with or are persuaded by a post.

Most Americans get news on the big general platforms, not on the one Trump owns.

In 2026, 40% of US adults regularly get news on Facebook, 34% on YouTube, 23% on Instagram and 20% on TikTok. X stands at 10% and Truth Social at 2%. Pew finds Republicans slightly more likely than Democrats to get news on Facebook and X, and Democrats more likely on Instagram, TikTok and Reddit. YouTube is even.

His playbook now travels across parties.

Senior figures in both parties have adopted parts of it. Pew's study of Congress found that mentioning the other side's leading figures lifted engagement for Democrats and Republicans alike, consistent with our finding that opponent posts beat his usual. The cases below cover both parties: two Democrats who borrowed parts of his style, evidence from Congress on both sides, and Trump's own move to a platform he owns. The follower snapshot above covers three senior figures from each party.

Governor of California · Democrat

Gavin Newsom's press office copied the tone

From August 2025 the office's X account adopted what ABC7 described as "all caps, MAGA phrases" and personal praise in Trump's style. The office reported 250,000 new followers that month and an engagement rate above 50%. A UC Berkeley poll for the LA Times found 59% of California voters wanted him to keep going.

Figures from Newsom's office, reported by ABC7 Los Angeles, August 2025.

Mayor of New York City · Democrat

Zohran Mamdani copied the directness, on video

His 2025 campaign relied on direct-to-camera short videos filmed on the street, including one explaining ranked-choice voting in Hindi. By the June 2025 primary he had more than 1 million Instagram followers, and one "Subway Takes" TikTok passed 3 million views.

NBC News, 26 June 2025.

Members of Congress · both parties

Opponents drive engagement on both sides

Posts mentioning prominent figures from the other party received "a substantial boost" in engagement for Democrats and Republicans alike. The median Republican member got more engagement per post; Democrats got 73% of all likes on Twitter in early 2020, because they posted more and had more large accounts.

Pew Research Center, 16 July 2020.

Trump himself · Republican

Owned platform: control over reach

Truth Social lets him post without depending on another company's rules and keeps his audience on his own platform. The trade-off in his own data: a typical post earns about a fifth of a typical first-term tweet, and only 2% of adults use the platform for news.

Datasets provided by ADI; Pew Research Center, 2026.

What can other politicians learn?

Eight lessons that hold for social media in 2026

These are communication tactics measured in Trump's data, set against how platforms and audiences work today. They describe what draws engagement, not whether any message is right. Each comes with the evidence and the main caution.

Trump's 2024 coalition was broader than his 2016 one, so one feed cannot reach all of it.

Pew's study of validated 2024 voters shows the coalition widened. White voters without a degree fell from 63% to 51% of his voters between 2016 and 2024. Voters under 50 rose to 40%. Hispanic voters rose from 6% to 10%, and men under 50 split almost evenly after backing Biden by ten points in 2020.
